# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 IDPs and coupled folding-and-binding

Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) lack a stable folded structure but execute essential functions in signalling, transcription, and the assembly of membraneless organelles (Wright and Dyson, 1999; van der Lee et al., 2014). Around 40 to 50 percent of eukaryotic proteins contain a disordered region of at least 30 residues, and disordered regions are enriched in cancer-associated proteins and viral interaction hubs (Ward et al., 2004; Iakoucheva et al., 2002). Function prediction for IDPs is hard for two reasons: the sequence-structure-function chain is broken at the middle link, and functional signal sits in short motifs and composition biases rather than conserved catalytic residues (Davey et al., 2012).

The mechanism I focus on is coupled folding-and-binding, in which a disordered region undergoes a **disorder-to-order (d2o) transition** on binding a partner (Dyson and Wright, 2002). This underlies transcription factor and coactivator interactions (Radhakrishnan et al., 1997), kinase-substrate recognition (Bah et al., 2015), and tumour suppressor networks like p53 (Oldfield et al., 2008). Short binding-competent d2o segments are called MoRFs (Mohan et al., 2006), and MoRF prediction has been a target of the field for over a decade (Disfani et al., 2012). I use the DisProt database (Quaglia et al., 2022) as the reference curated resource; the IDPO term `IDPO:0000011` (Structural transition; disorder-to-order) is my operational d2o label.

## 1.2 Two candidate information sources for d2o prediction

Two data sources could plausibly help predict d2o from sequence. The first is **Gene Ontology annotations**, organized into three sub-ontologies: Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC) (Ashburner et al., 2000). CAFA benchmarks show that combining sequence similarity with machine learning and GO context beats sequence-only methods on the general proteome (Radivojac et al., 2013; Kulmanov and Hoehndorf, 2020), but the gap shrinks on the disordered slice (Nair et al., 2022). The second is **protein language models** like ESM-2 (Lin et al., 2023) and ProstT5 (Heinzinger et al., 2023), pretrained on hundreds of millions of sequences with self-supervised objectives that encode structural, functional, and evolutionary information (Rives et al., 2021; Elnaggar et al., 2022).

The specific question this study answers: given a modern PLM baseline, does adding GO context improve d2o prediction, or has the PLM already absorbed the discriminative content GO carries?

## 1.3 Study design and contributions

I test this with a pre-registered 2×2×2 factorial across the three GO sub-ontologies added on top of an ESM-2 baseline. All cross-validation uses GroupKFold on CD-HIT clusters at 40% sequence identity, which blocks the sequence-similarity leakage that inflates most protein function benchmarks. I test robustness across three prediction scales (whole-protein, disorder-pool, per-region), two PLM families (ESM-2, ProstT5), and two classifier families (Random Forest, XGBoost). The primary metric is AUPRC, which handles the 15% positive-class prevalence better than ROC-AUC (Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015).

I pre-registered four hypotheses: H1-H3 that each sub-ontology main effect (BP, MF, CC) is positive; H4 that GO helps most where the sequence baseline is least confident. This report makes four contributions. First, a cluster-controlled factorial benchmark for the specific GO-plus-PLM question on DisProt d2o. Second, the finding that **PLM embeddings already contain the discriminative content of GO Slim** (99.7%/0.3% importance split with biologically coherent top GO features). Third, the identification of sequence representation and prediction scale as the strongest lever on performance (each scale step contributes about +0.037 AUPRC). Fourth, evidence that the null generalizes to **higher-resolution GO** representations, ruling out the interpretation that GO Slim was too coarse.
